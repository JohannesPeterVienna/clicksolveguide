Guided diagnoses

Each diagnosis asks one question at a time. Every answer crosses layers off the map until the likely culprit is left – then you see only the steps that fit, for your system.

The diagnoses run entirely in your browser. Your answers and notes are kept in this browser's local storage so you can come back after restarting the router; nothing is sent to us or anyone else. At any point you can download the fix log as a text file or print it. It lists the checks you did, your answers, the result and the recommended steps with their menu paths – the kind of record that saves time with an internet provider's support line, a company help desk or a repair shop.

Every step carries a risk label. “Harmless” steps change nothing. “Changes a setting” comes with the undo. “Admin needed” means you will be asked for an administrator password. “Last resort” steps remove saved settings and only appear after the gentler checks. If a diagnosis reaches a security warning – for example a certificate warning on a website – it stops and tells you what is safe to do. It never explains how to click past such a warning.

Not sure which one to pick? If nothing loads at all, start with Wi-Fi connected but no internet. If only one address fails, start with one website won't load.

When each check fits

The six diagnoses overlap on purpose – a printer that is “offline” can be a Wi-Fi problem, and a Chrome error can be a router problem. What decides the right starting point is the first thing you see on screen. The paragraphs below say which symptom each diagnosis starts from, what its first question is, and when another page is the better choice.

Wi-Fi connected but no internet: guided diagnosis

Use this when your device shows a Wi-Fi connection – bars, sometimes “No internet, secured” – but pages and apps don’t load. The first question is whether everything fails or only one site or app, because that single answer clears four of the five layers when only one service is affected. The tree then covers sign-in pages on hotel and public networks, a stuck connection on the device, VPN or filter software, name lookup, the router and a provider outage, for Windows, macOS, iPhone, iPad and Android.

“This site can’t be reached” in Chrome: what it means and what to check

Use this when Chrome shows its grey “This site can’t be reached” page. The first question is the small code under the headline – NXDOMAIN, TIMED_OUT, REFUSED, RESET or none – because each code points to a different layer, from a typing mistake in the address to a DNS server or a filter on your network. In Edge, Safari or Firefox the codes look different: start with the one-website diagnosis when only one address fails, or with Wi-Fi connected but no internet when nothing loads.

One website won’t load but others do

Use this when one address fails while other websites load normally, in any browser. The first question is what the page shows: an error, a blank or endlessly loading page, a block notice from your network, or a security warning. A certificate warning is a hard stop – the diagnosis lists only safe actions there. The other paths compare a private window or second browser, another device on the same Wi-Fi, mobile data and the site’s official status page to separate the website, your browser data or extensions, the device and the network.

Printer shows “Offline” in Windows 11: step by step

Use this when Windows 11 or 10 lists your printer as “Offline” or jobs wait in the queue without printing. The first question is whether the printer itself is on, awake and free of error messages on its display, because no setting on the PC fixes a printer that shows an error. After that, the checks separate a USB cable, a printer that has dropped off your Wi-Fi, the router, a stuck queue or offline setting, and a hung Print Spooler.

Printer not printing from a Mac: guided checks

Use this when a Mac doesn’t print: the queue is paused, the printer is “not connected”, or jobs disappear without a page coming out. The first question is whether the printer still appears in Printers & Scanners. From there the tree reads the queue status line, checks the USB or Wi-Fi connection and compares with printing from an iPhone or iPad. Removing and re-adding the printer comes late, and resetting the printing system is the last resort – each with its undo described.

Laptop won’t connect to Wi-Fi

Use this when the problem happens before the internet even starts: a Windows laptop or MacBook can’t see your network, sees it but won’t join, or rejects the password. The first question is which of those you see. If the laptop does join but then shows “No internet”, the diagnosis says so and sends you to the Wi-Fi-but-no-internet check, because the join itself is then working.
